The Same Wall, Six Times

An audit that kept finding the same failure

David Lee Wise

What this is

Front Matter

This is the record of one working session. It began with a request to audit a paper posted to a discussion forum and ended with a small language model trained on a transcript. Nothing in between was planned.

The session produced thirty-two findings with numbers attached. Every one is stamped LIT, meaning it was either reproduced by a script whose output appears in the text, or it sits in a public court record. Nothing here is stamped from memory. Where a claim is interpretive rather than measured it is marked AMBER and the reason is given.

The book has one argument, and it was not chosen in advance. It emerged because the same failure kept appearing in unrelated systems: a metric computed inside a system stops tracking the thing outside it, and the system cannot detect this from inside. It showed up in a cognitive architecture, in a random walk, in an embedding, in a glyph-design search, in an attention stack, and in my own reasoning about all five. Six times, one shape.

A note on the second author. Roughly half of what follows was produced by a language model during the session, including every script and every number. That is not a disclaimer, it is one of the findings: the corpus the final model trained on was itself machine-generated text about this conversation, and the model reading it back to itself is the argument of the last chapter, arrived at accidentally.

The chapters are short because the findings are short. Padding a result is the specific error the first chapter is about.

Six Gates

Chapter 1

The paper proposed something called Recursive Cognitive Refinement: a primary layer generates an interpretation, a reflective sublayer revises it, and the loop runs until the two agree. The author wanted help getting it into the right hands.

Six gates were applied. Claim extraction: separate the load-bearing assertions from the prose carrying them. Six claims emerged, of which three were proposals rather than results. Falsifiability: for each claim, what observation would kill it? The central hypothesis had none — emergent cognition can arise from recursive sublayers has no negative case. Novelty: the paper had zero citations. Not a thin bibliography; no reference section at all, in a document claiming to open a new research direction.

Math verification is where it came apart. The coherence metric was defined as one minus a semantic divergence between the primary and reflective states, with convergence declared when coherence approaches one. Set the reflective transform to the identity function and divergence is zero, coherence is one at iteration zero, and the error is exactly what it started as. The objective function is maximised by switching the reflective layer off.

Then a twelve-line simulation, with memory initialised distinct from the primary state so the setup was not rigged:

 n       K         |P-truth|
 0    -1.10000     4.70000
 3     0.75294     2.88340
 7     0.98576     2.65514
11     0.99918     2.64198

Coherence converged to 0.99918. The error plateaued at 2.64198. The loop found a stable, self-consistent fixed point unrelated to the truth, and by its own metric it had succeeded completely.

A grep of the algorithm for ground truth, feedback, error, reward, verify, external, and label returned absent for every term. The only quality signal in the system was agreement with itself.

The notation ratio gate and the right hands gate followed quickly. Ten symbols carrying one idea, and no result to hand over: the benchmark was proposed, the language was proposed, the comparison was proposed. A commenter in the thread had already got there in nine words — don't propose the work, do the work.

The Undefined Letter

Chapter 2

Everything in the paper rested on a distance function between two meanings, and the paper never said what it was. One capital letter, undefined, carrying the entire contribution.

The obvious cheap choice fails immediately. Take a claim and its flat contradiction, then the same claim paraphrased:

CONTRADICTION  D = 0.200   K = +0.800
PARAPHRASE     D = 0.900   K = +0.100

One inserted word flips the meaning entirely and scores 80% coherent. A sentence meaning precisely the same thing in different words scores 10%. Any system running this metric converges happily onto contradictions and rejects its own correct restatements as incoherent. Swap in embedding cosine and the disease survives in better clothes, because negation is nearly invisible to sentence embeddings — the two statements share topic, entities, register and syntax, differing only in the part that matters.

Four families are available. Surface overlap: blind to negation, punished by paraphrase. Embedding cosine: same hole. Distributional divergence over output logits: measures the model's state rather than the claim. Bidirectional entailment: correct, two statements being equivalent when each entails the other — and it collapses to a near-discrete metric, which destroys the continuous space the convergence proof requires.

Get the distance semantically right and you break the calculus around it. That tension is the interesting problem in the paper, and the paper never reaches it, because it never asks what the letter means.

Underneath all four: every one compares the system's output to the system's other output. You can make the metric as sophisticated as you like and it still only ever reports that the system agrees with itself.

Coordinates, Not Content

Chapter 3

A weight is usually explained as a learned parameter, a number multiplied by an input. True and nearly useless.

Three layers down. A weight matrix row is a vector in the input space, so a layer is a set of learned directions and the activation reports how much the input points along each. That is layer one. A weight's value is the accumulated sum of every gradient pull from every training example that touched it, which is why no single example can be read back out. That is layer two.

Layer three is the one worth having, and it is demonstrable. Take a small network, permute its hidden units, and permute the weight rows and columns to match:

permuted:  max |f - f'| = 0.00e+00   dW/W = 1.254   cos = +0.213
rescaled:  max |f - f'| = 8.88e-16   dW/W = 6.300   cos = +1.000

The permuted network's weights are 125% different by norm with cosine similarity 0.213 — barely correlated — and the function is identical to the last bit. Not approximately. Exactly zero error. The rescaled version is 630% off and identical to floating-point noise.

For a four-unit layer there are 24 permutations. For a width-4096 layer there are 4096 factorial, times a continuous family of rescalings. The weight vector you hold is one arbitrary point in an astronomically large equivalence class, all computing the same function.

Which is why weight-space distance is nearly useless for comparing models, why averaging two independently trained networks produces garbage, and why interpretability cannot proceed by reading weights off directly. You would be reading coordinates, and the coordinates were chosen by the random seed.

The function is the object. The weights are one description of it.

What a Cut Costs

Chapter 4

If weights are coordinates, you would at least hope each hidden unit meant something. Mostly it does not. Networks pack more features than they have dimensions and store them in overlapping, non-orthogonal directions. There is no cat weight. There is a cat direction, and it is not axis-aligned.

This has a consequence for anyone who imagines removing something from a trained model. Sixty-four features packed into sixteen dimensions, then one feature removed by projecting out its direction:

target feature: retained -0.0%  -> removed
collateral on the other 63:
   mean damage    4.7%
   max  damage   23.6%
   damaged >10%:  12 / 63
   damaged  >1%:  39 / 63

The target went cleanly. Thirty-nine of the remaining sixty-three were damaged, twelve by more than ten percent, one by nearly a quarter. And that is a toy with a packing ratio of four to one. Scale the ratio up and the smearing gets worse.

There is no scalpel. Which is why machine unlearning remains an open problem rather than a feature, and why what happens in practice is suppression at the behaviour layer while the representation persists underneath. Different mechanism, different failure modes, and worth not collapsing the two.

It also closes off a tempting inference. If you cannot see inside a model, you might try to reconstruct what was removed from the shape of what remains. But cuts do not leave clean holes. They leave uneven smearing that looks like ordinary variation everywhere else. You cannot read the excision off the residue.

Seven Million Copies

Chapter 5

The session turned, for a while, to where corpora come from. This chapter is the public record, and it is the only chapter whose numbers came from a court rather than a script.

In June 2025 a federal judge held that digitising lawfully purchased print books to train a model was transformative and constituted fair use. The same order held that downloading from shadow libraries to build a permanent digital library did not. The scope in the summary judgment: roughly five million books from one library, two million from another, seven million copies in total, plus a separate pirated dataset of 196,000 books that formed the basis of the original complaint.

A class was certified for the piracy only, not for the training. The filter required an ISBN or ASIN and registration with the Copyright Office within five years of publication and before the download. Roughly 500,000 of the seven million qualified — 7.1%. Final approval came at $1.5 billion, about $3,000 per book across more than 400,000 titles, with no admission of liability. The remaining 6.5 million rightsholders received nothing, and the 196,000 were excluded on a metadata technicality.

Two observations that the coverage tends to drop. First, the class filter systematically excludes foreign editions and pre-ISBN material, so it under-represents exactly the categories that were already thin. Second, statutory damages run from $750 to $150,000 per work. One person downloading one work has effectively zero practical exposure and a five-figure nominal one. Seven million works is the same act at a scale where the arithmetic changes character: scale converted a prohibition into a line item.

And the remedy operates on the wrong object. A payment resolves a claim. It does not unmake a model, and per the previous chapter it cannot be surgically undone. Model outputs become web text, web text becomes training data, distillation moves it between models directly, and provenance dissolves after one hop.

The Loop That Agreed With Itself

Chapter 6

An embedder was built from scratch over a small corpus: count the character transitions, weight them by pointwise mutual information, form the Gram matrix so similarity is measured over rows rather than adjacency, factor it, keep the top components. Then the associations were read out.

weights        -> part, activations, coordinates
signal         -> external, means, only, itself
superposition  -> single, things, directions, unrelated

Those are the right answers. A screenshot of that table would look like a working embedder.

Then the corpus was split in half and the whole procedure run twice on disjoint data.

mean top-5 neighbour overlap   0.029
chance level for 5 of 299      0.0167
median overlap                 0.000
words with zero overlap        259 / 299

Two hundred and fifty-nine of two hundred and ninety-nine words shared no neighbours at all between the two runs. Four shared two. Nothing shared three. The sensible-looking table was noise that happened to land on plausible words, and nothing but training on data the first run had never seen could have revealed it.

The reason is volume. Two thousand two hundred and thirty-five tokens, nine hundred and ten types, sixty-seven percent of them appearing exactly once. A single conversation is not a language, it is a transcript. But that diagnosis came later. What came first was the discovery that the output looked correct and was not, and the only instrument that could tell the difference was an external one.

Which is Chapter 1 again, in a different system, found by accident.

Argmax and the Spinor

Chapter 7

A small agent was given a fixed grid of letters and a transition table counted from that same grid. At each step it read the letter beneath it, fetched that letter's row, scored its eight neighbours by transition probability, and moved to the best one. Pure greedy choice, fully deterministic.

400 steps -> 7 distinct cells visited, 0.6% of the container
path: "he pre pre pre pre pre pre pre pre pre p"

It locked into a six-cell cycle almost immediately. Not a bug. The transition table has 212 nonzero cells of 729, so most neighbours score exactly zero, and argmax never takes a lower-probability step — leaving a local cycle of high-frequency pairs requires exactly the move it will not make.

The escape was built as a spinor. The agent carries a phase that advances by a random increment and wraps at 4π rather than 2π; heading is half the phase, so one full turn of heading costs two full turns of phase, and the sign inverts halfway. Rotate it once and it returns inverted; twice and it returns itself. Three planes of motion were selected by where the phase sat: a probability-weighted local step, a jump through token space to any cell holding a sampled letter, and a ballistic move along the heading that ignores probability entirely.

argmax  400 steps -> 7 cells   (0.6%)
spinor  400 steps -> 330 cells (30.6%)

Forty-seven times the coverage. And the important detail: the local plane still uses the same probabilities that trapped argmax. What breaks the lock is the ballistic and jump planes — the moves that ignore the internal metric. The escape did not come from better scoring. It came from admitting a term the loop does not generate.

A Real Qubit

Chapter 8

This chapter is a detour that turned into a load-bearing result.

A spin-½ system was implemented properly: state as a Bloch vector, exact rotation about a magnetic field for Larmor precession, Born-rule measurement, projective collapse. Five tests before anything was drawn. Norm preservation held to 1.8e-13 over ten thousand steps. Measured probability along an arbitrary axis came to 0.7266 against an analytic 0.7268 over two hundred thousand samples. Singlet correlations matched minus the dot product at every angle tested. And the CHSH sum reached 2.827 against a classical ceiling of 2 and a quantum bound of 2.828.

The fifth test failed first, at 0.006 instead of 2.828. The angles were wrong — both of one party's settings placed symmetrically about the other's, which cancels the four terms exactly. Then the patch to fix it silently matched nothing, because two variables shared a line, and the identical wrong answer came back. The second attempt asserted the patch target before writing. The physics test caught the physics error; the assertion caught the tooling error. Neither would have caught the other.

Then the finding that mattered. Prepare a Bell state and both Bloch vectors collapse to the origin. That is not a rendering failure: each half of a maximally entangled pair is maximally mixed and has no direction of its own. Nothing is left locally. Everything is in the correlation.

So two agents were entangled and set to walk the same corpus, their measurement outcomes steering their headings.

Bell pair    <AB> -0.354   coverage 847   divergence 0.1805
independent  <AB>  0.001   coverage 953   divergence 0.1649

The correlation is real and the sampling is worse. Correlated walkers cover less ground than independent ones, and their learned tables end up further apart. Entanglement bought a statistical relationship no observer of either single path could detect, and bought nothing at all for the task.

One Line

Chapter 9

A question was posed: could you design an alphabet where each letter is a single line, as simple as possible, and still be machine-readable?

It was answered by search rather than by drawing. Every polyline on a three-by-three lattice with at most two segments — 576 of them, no pen lifts, no immediate reversals, no repeated segment — was enumerated, rasterised, and twenty-seven selected by greedy maximum-minimum diversity. Mean strokes per letter: 1.59. Eleven letters came out as a bare straight line with no corner, and those were assigned to the eleven commonest symbols, spending strokes where they are used least.

Then two identical classifiers, same architecture and training budget, one on the designed alphabet and one on real Latin letterforms under identical distortion:

              sev0.5  sev1.0  sev1.5  sev2.0
monoline       100.0    96.5    66.7    38.3
Latin           97.9    89.4    42.8    14.9

And it is not an artifact of the selection resolution — the gap is +23.2, +28.0 and +31.2 points at ten, sixteen and twenty-four pixels, widening rather than closing. Latin's failures cluster in the thin-vertical family; the designed alphabet's are different pairs entirely.

Two refinements were tried and neither worked. Doubling the stroke budget moved accuracy less than run-to-run noise. And the constraint that turned out to matter was one nobody had questioned: requiring a single continuous stroke. Two disjoint lines reach 74.7% where one continuous stroke of the same length reaches 66.7% — a corner constrains where the second segment can go, and separate lines do not.

The constraint had been imposed in the first sentence of the problem and carried unexamined for six experiments.

The Metric That Lied

Chapter 10

The glyphs in the previous chapter were selected by maximising the minimum pairwise distance between their clean rasters. It was the obvious objective and it was wrong.

lattice         minD    acc@1.5
3x3, 2 seg     0.404     65.0%
4x3, 2 seg     0.477     63.6%
4x4, 2 seg     0.543     62.2%

Minimum distance climbs thirty-four percent while accuracy falls. They move in opposite directions. A finer lattice buys cleaner separation on undistorted rasters and spends it immediately on fragility, because the glyphs start relying on spatial detail that rotation and rescaling destroy.

The same thing happened again when extra channels were tested. Curve was the confident prediction — a curved stroke stays curved under rotation, so it should have been the robust addition. On single-segment glyphs it made things worse, 77.9% against 82.6%, while raising clean minimum distance from 0.281 to 0.670. Highest separation, worst accuracy.

What did work was the channel predicted to fail. Ink intensity gave +7.6 points for no extra strokes and survived ±35% pressure variation, because intensity is the only channel an affine transform cannot touch. Rotation, scale, shear and translation all preserve brightness. Curve and width live in the same space as the thing being corrupted; depth does not.

The principle: a free channel only stays free if it is orthogonal to the distortion group.

The methodological finding is larger. Minimum distance is computed entirely inside the representation. Accuracy under distortion requires generating data the selection process never saw. Optimise the first and you can move confidently away from the second for as long as you like, and nothing inside the procedure will tell you.

Building the Stack

Chapter 11

Six stages, all inspectable. A fixed corpus. A twenty-seven symbol tokeniser. An embedder — transition counts, pointwise mutual information, Gram matrix, eigendecomposition, eight dimensions. Eight attention heads over a thirteen-token window. A learned readout. A sampler. Then it reads its own output back in.

The heads were constructed rather than learned, and the reason is a finding. The first attempt used random query and key projections, which is what an untrained transformer has:

all 8 heads: entropy 2.551 - 2.562, against a 2.565 ceiling

Flat. Featureless. Honest: attention at initialisation has no structure. So eight heads were specified to do jobs that trained transformers are documented to develop — a previous-token shift, an attention sink on position zero, a same-character matcher, two class routers, a content-similarity head, a bigram-plausibility head, and a uniform baseline. Entropy then spanned 0.016 to 1.735.

The first working version lost to a bigram table: perplexity 14.17 against 10.38, accuracy 25.7% against 30.4%. Attention averages embeddings, which destroys the exact-identity signal a count table reads straight off the shelf. The leave-one-head-out was worse news than the headline — dropping six of the eight heads improved held-out loss. Only the same-character head earned its place.

Adding direct token identity brought it to a tie: 10.34 against 10.38. And a question about whether the same-character head was searching for a matching prefix produced a clean negative. Reading the actual attention on the seed window: the query character occurred exactly once, so the head found no earlier match and put weight 1.00 on itself. A lookup that found only the thing doing the looking.

A proper two-step induction head was then built — match the prefix, copy the successor. It fired correctly, 5.13 matches per step at the longest context tested. Perplexity got worse at every context length. The hypothesis that it needed to copy exact identity rather than smoothed embeddings was tested and also wrong. Eighty-one extra features on eight hundred and fifty-three training rows: the model was not failing to express induction, it was failing to estimate it.

Fifteen Thousand Tokens

Chapter 12

The question was finally asked properly: what corpus size would work? A scaling curve answered it, and corrected four earlier statements in the process.

train tokens    bigram   trigram  |  stack
       1,000    14.463    16.504  | 18.207
       6,000    12.698    11.676  | 12.997
      15,000    12.383    10.477  | 12.132
      40,000    11.465     8.147  | 10.206
     250,000    11.177     7.167  |  9.639

The stack passes the bigram at roughly fifteen thousand tokens. The bigram saturates almost immediately — 14.5 to 11.2 across a two hundred and fifty fold increase in data. But the trigram is pulling away, and the stack never catches it.

So the architecture was tested against the data. At eighty thousand tokens, replacing the linear readout with a single hidden layer took perplexity from 10.029 to 8.017 — most of the gap to the trigram, closed by nonlinearity rather than by data. And the heads did earn their place once there was a nonlinearity to use them: heads plus a hidden layer beat raw context plus the same hidden layer, 8.02 against 9.35.

Four separate times in the session the corpus had been named as the bottleneck. At one thousand and eighty tokens it genuinely was. Past fifteen thousand the binding constraint had become the linear readout, and the attribution kept going to data because a single measurement at one corpus size cannot distinguish the two. Only the curve can, and the curve had not been run.

With a hundred and eighty-five thousand tokens and a nonlinear readout, the stack finally beat the trigram: 5.274 against 5.619, accuracy 48.3%, train perplexity 5.058 against test 5.274 — the memorisation gone. A 4-gram table still won at 3.855. Pure memorisation remains the thing to beat at this scale, and the point where attention's context reach repays its sample inefficiency is still far above anything measured here.

The Corpus Was Me

Chapter 13

The corpus that everything up to the scaling test used — the embedder, the split-half test, the walker, the spinor, the alphabet, the attention heads, the agent — was one thousand and eighty characters of prose written during the session by the language model, summarising the session. Lowercased, stripped to letters and spaces. It opens: the paper proposes recursive cognitive refinement.

The entire pipeline was trained on machine-generated text about itself. The agent producing the paper pros funronre was reading a summary of the model-collapse argument back to itself. That was not arranged. It is simply what the file was, and it went unstated for most of the session.

The second corpus was a transcript of a different collaboration, roughly 99.6% machine-generated — 714 characters of human framing and 184,198 of model output. Measured against human-written prose at matched size it carried lower character entropy, lower conditional entropy, twenty-six percent fewer word types, and slower vocabulary growth. The collapse signature, with numbers.

Except the vocabulary comparison was size-confounded, and a third corpus exposed it. Look at where the type counts cross:

tokens   AI types   human types   ratio
   500       250         243       1.029
 2,000       649         553       1.174
 8,000     1,436       1,771       0.811
32,000     3,004       4,307       0.697

The ratio crosses one around three thousand tokens. Below that the machine text is more diverse. Only above does it fall behind, and then keeps falling. Measured over the same range, Heaps' exponent is 0.578 against 0.747 — the gap is real. But the correct description is not impoverishment. It is earlier saturation: it starts rich and stops accumulating. A single-size comparison had picked one point on two curves that cross.

And a specification document, heavily templated, showed 12.5% repeated five-grams — triple the human text — while having the highest perplexity of the three. Repetition and predictability came apart. So low entropy is not a signature of machine origin. That inference, made confidently one chapter earlier, does not hold.

Genre remains unresolved. Fiction against technical documentation is a real confound. The measurement that would settle it — human-written fiction, a hundred thousand characters, single author — was never available.

The Same Wall, Six Times

Coda

Six systems, none related, one failure.

A cognitive architecture converged to coherence 0.99918 at an error of 2.64198. A greedy walker locked into seven cells of a thousand and eighty. An embedder produced correct-looking associations with a split-half overlap of 0.029 against a chance floor of 0.0167. A glyph search raised minimum separation by thirty-four percent while accuracy fell. An attention stack tied a bigram at thirty times the parameter count and had six of eight components actively hurting. And in the middle of all of it, four confident attributions to corpus size that a scaling curve later reassigned to architecture.

In every case the internal metric was rising. In every case something outside the loop was required to notice, and in every case that external thing was cheap: twelve lines of Python, a second training run on held-out data, a distortion the selection process never saw, one more point on a curve.

The pattern has a formal ancestor. A consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency — if it could, it would be inconsistent. Hilbert wanted mathematics to certify itself from the inside, and the answer arrived in ten pages. It also has a less formal ancestor: a private criterion of correctness is not a criterion, because there is no difference between being right and seeming right to yourself. Both were available before any of the code was written, and neither would have produced a single number.

The practical form is smaller than the philosophy. Before optimising a metric, spend one measurement finding out whether it tracks the thing you want. That measurement has to come from outside the system computing the metric, and it is almost always cheaper than the optimisation it protects.

Thirty-two findings. The session was not looking for any of them.
