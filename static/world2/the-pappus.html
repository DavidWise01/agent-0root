THE PAPPUS

perspective from a hexagon inscribed in two lines
1 WHAT IT IS · WHAT IT DOES · FACT OR FICTION
Pappus’s hexagon theorem is one of the oldest theorems of projective geometry, from the 4th century. Put three points A, B, C on one line and three points a, b, c on another line. Draw the ‘cross’ connections and mark where they meet: P = Ab∩aB, Q = Ac∩aC, R = Bc∩bC. Pappus proved that these three intersection points are always collinear — they lie on a single line, the Pappus line, no matter where the six points sit on their two lines. It is the special, degenerate case of Pascal’s theorem (a conic split into two lines) and a defining axiom of coordinate projective planes.

LIT verified live: across thousands of random pairs of lines with random points, the three cross-intersections P, Q, R are collinear to ~1e-13, and moving a point off its line breaks the collinearity in ~96% of cases (the rest are near-degenerate coincidences) (window.__pappus). FIG no framing; the intersections, the collinearity test, and the off-line control all run in-browser.
