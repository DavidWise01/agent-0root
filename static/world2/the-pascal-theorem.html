THE PASCAL THEOREM

six points on a conic whose opposite sides meet on one line
1 WHAT IT IS · WHAT IT DOES · FACT OR FICTION
Pascal’s theorem — the ‘mystic hexagram’, found by Blaise Pascal at sixteen — is a jewel of projective geometry. Take any six points on a conic (a circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola) and join them in order into a hexagon. Extend the three pairs of opposite sides until each pair meets. The theorem: those three intersection points always lie on a single straight line, the Pascal line. It holds no matter how the six points are placed or labelled, and it is purely projective — only incidence matters, not distance or angle. Its projective dual is Brianchon’s theorem.

LIT verified live: for tens of thousands of random hexagons inscribed in an ellipse, the three opposite-side intersection points are collinear — the triangle they form has area (normalized) below 1e-6 (window.__pascal). FIG no framing; the six conic points, the three intersections, and their collinearity are computed independently in-browser.
